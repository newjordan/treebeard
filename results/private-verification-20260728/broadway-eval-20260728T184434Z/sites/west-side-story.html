West Side Story

Original Broadway Production • 1957
A Romeo & Juliet Retelling for the Urban Age

Overview

Premiering on September 26, 1957, at the Winter Garden Theatre, West Side Story stands as a monumental achievement in American musical theatre. It reimagined Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet for a gritty, mid-century New York City, replacing the feud between noble houses with the violent turf war between two teenage street gangs: the Jets (white Americans) and the Sharks (Puerto Rican immigrants).

Unlike the romanticized musicals of the previous decade, this production was dark, socially conscious, and visually distinct. It marked the first time that dance was not merely an interlude, but the primary vehicle for storytelling and character development.

The Creative Team

The production is often cited as the perfect alignment of four distinct artistic geniuses.

  • Music: Leonard Bernstein
  • Lyrics: Stephen Sondheim (his Broadway debut)
  • Book: Arthur Laurents
  • Choreography & Direction: Jerome Robbins
  • Orchestration: Sid Ramin and Irwin Kostal

Setting & Story

The Setting

The story is set in the mid-1950s on the Upper West Side of Manhattan. The backdrop is a tenement playground, a place of refuge for the Jets, which is threatened by the arrival of the Sharks. The urban landscape is cold, concrete, and dangerous, contrasting sharply with the warmth of the Sharks' family life.

The Narrative

The narrative follows Tony, a former leader of the Jets, and Maria, the sister of Sharks leader Bernardo. Their love blossoms amidst escalating gang violence. The play culminates in a tragic rumble and the deaths of key characters, mirroring the tragedy of Verona but grounded in the socio-political tensions of 1950s America.

Dance & Music Innovation

Jerome Robbins utilized dance as a narrative device. The "Cool" scene, for example, uses the tension of a dance-off to convey the threat of violence without a single punch being thrown. The choreography blended ballet, jazz, and ethnic folk dances to distinguish the cultural identities of the gangs.

Leonard Bernstein composed a score that was symphonic in scope. He utilized dissonance and complex rhythms (including a 5/4 time signature in "America") to reflect the chaotic energy of the city. Stephen Sondheim, in his first major commission, provided lyrics that were witty, cynical, and deeply emotional, establishing his signature style immediately.

Legacy

The original production ran for 732 performances, a significant run for the time, and was immediately hailed as a classic. It broke the mold of the "integrated musical," where song, dance, and plot are inseparable.

Its influence is immeasurable. It paved the way for the "concept musicals" of the 1960s and 70s (such as Company and Sweeney Todd, also by Sondheim). The 1961 film adaptation won 10 Academy Awards, cementing its place in pop culture history. Today, it remains one of the most frequently produced musicals in the world.
