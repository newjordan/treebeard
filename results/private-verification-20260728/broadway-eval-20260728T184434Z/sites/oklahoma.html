Oklahoma!

The Land of the Blue Corn

Music by Richard Rodgers • Lyrics & Book by Oscar Hammerstein II

Overview

Oklahoma! is the first musical written by the duo of Rodgers and Hammerstein. It premiered on Broadway at the St. James Theatre on March 31, 1943. The musical is based on Lynn Riggs' 1931 play Green Grow the Lilacs.

Running for 2,212 performances, it broke the record previously held by Uncle Tom's Cabin for the longest run in Broadway history. It is widely considered to be the first "integrated" musical—where the songs, dance, and plot all move forward the story, rather than serving as mere diversion.

Plot Synopsis

Set in the Oklahoma Territory around 1906, the story centers on the rivalry between two cowboys, Curly McLain and Willie Sharp, for the affections of the beautiful farm girl Laurey Williams.

Act I

Laurey is torn between the sweet, sincere Curly and the sinister, brooding farmhand Jud Fry. To make Curly jealous, she accepts an invitation to a box social from Jud, but Curly eventually wins her back with the help of her friend Ado Annie. The act ends with the "Dream Ballet," a surreal sequence visualizing Laurey's internal conflict.

Act II

Tensions rise as the community prepares for a barn raising. Jud, consumed by jealousy, attacks Curly. In a tragic turn of events, Curly is found dead, and Jud is arrested. The musical concludes with the community uniting as Oklahoma is admitted to the Union, symbolizing the triumph of community over individual malice.

Historical Impact

The Integrated Musical

Before Oklahoma!, musicals were often loose collections of sketches and songs (revues). Rodgers and Hammerstein changed this by ensuring every song and dance number advanced the plot or developed character. The famous Dream Ballet by Agnes de Mille, for instance, revealed the heroine's psychological state without a single word of dialogue.

Cultural Resonance

Opening during World War II, the musical offered a vision of American optimism, unity, and patriotism. The song "Oh, What a Beautiful Mornin'" became an anthem of hope. It solidified the model for future Broadway blockbusters like South Pacific, The King and I, and West Side Story.

Fun Facts

1. The Title: The title was not originally going to be "Oklahoma!". The working title was Away We Go! It was changed to "Oklahoma!" at the suggestion of a producer who felt the state name was more marketable.
2. The Dance: Agnes de Mille was hired to choreograph, but she refused to do the standard "ballet girls kicking their legs" style. She insisted on using dance to tell the story, which was revolutionary for the time.
3. The Curtain: The original curtain featured a painting of a prairie landscape. It was so large it had to be lowered by a crane.
